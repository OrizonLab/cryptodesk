The US crypto market now has a concrete regulatory process to watch — and it is not a new congressional vote.

On October 5, the Commodity Futures Trading Commission published an advance notice of proposed rulemaking covering **Regulation Crypto Asset Transactions (CTX)** and **Regulation Crypto Asset Markets (CAM)**. The notice asks for public comment on a purpose-built framework for certain retail crypto transactions, especially trades offered with margin, leverage or financing, and for exchanges that would want to operate under a CFTC registration model tailored to crypto markets.

That is a meaningful procedural step, but it is not a final rule. The document starts a rulemaking process, and the agency says comments will be due 60 days after publication in the Federal Register. For US readers, the practical question is not whether crypto suddenly became “regulated.” It is which venues, products and safeguards could eventually fit inside a federal framework — and what remains outside it.

## What the CFTC is actually proposing

The notice focuses on section 2(c)(2)(D) of the Commodity Exchange Act, the provision covering certain retail commodity transactions. In the agency’s initial design, a **crypto asset transaction**, or CTX, is a retail trade involving a crypto asset that is margined, leveraged or financed, subject to the statutory conditions.

The proposal also introduces the idea of a **crypto asset market**, or CAM. A CAM would be a new sub-category of designated contract market registration for an exchange that wants to offer CTXs under rules adapted to crypto’s market structure. An existing CFTC-registered exchange could potentially offer these products under tailored requirements, while a non-registrant could choose between ordinary DCM registration and the proposed CAM route.

This is an option, not a mandate for every US crypto platform. CFTC Chairman Michael S. Selig stressed that only Congress can require all crypto exchanges to register with the agency. The proposal would instead create a federal path for platforms that choose, or are required, to come under the CFTC’s jurisdiction for the products they offer.

## Why leverage is the centre of the proposal

Spot bitcoin ownership and leveraged trading create different risks. In a spot purchase, the customer generally pays for the asset and takes delivery or holds an entitlement through a custodian. In a margined position, a relatively small amount of collateral controls a much larger exposure. A sharp move can trigger forced liquidation, amplify losses and transmit stress across venues.

The CFTC’s proposed framework is aimed at the second category. It would address how exchanges list CTXs, monitor markets, protect customer property, manage conflicts, maintain systems and handle abusive practices. The agency also discusses an interpretation of “actual delivery” under which transferring a crypto asset to a customer’s external, non-custodial wallet within 28 days would generally satisfy the statutory exception.

That point matters because the boundary between exchange-based leverage and delivery is central to the Commodity Exchange Act. The agency is attempting to make the boundary more operational without pretending that every crypto product has the same risk profile as a traditional futures contract.

## The proposed exchange model is more than a new label

A CAM would still have to meet the CFTC’s core principles for designated contract markets. Those principles cover areas such as contract listing, market surveillance, financial integrity, customer funds, conflicts of interest and system safeguards. The difference is that the implementing rules would be written with crypto-specific features in mind.

For listing decisions, the notice points to issues that do not map neatly onto older commodities: token distribution, ownership concentration, lock-up and vesting schedules, programmatic issuance or buybacks, and the possibility of manipulation through thin or fragmented markets.

The notice also contemplates a proof-of-reserves obligation for exchanges that hold customer property in omnibus accounts for customers’ benefit. That would not eliminate counterparty risk, and it would not make a platform immune from insolvency or operational failure. It would, however, move the discussion from broad assurances toward a defined regulatory obligation if the idea survives the comment and final-rule process.

## What changes for US customers — eventually

If adopted substantially as proposed, the framework could give US customers more visibility into the legal status and operating standards of venues offering leveraged crypto exposure. It could also give exchanges a route to offer products under a single federal regulator rather than navigating a patchwork of state licences for every activity.

But the timetable is important. An advance notice of proposed rulemaking is an early-stage document. It signals the agency’s intent and asks for input; it does not authorize a platform to launch a CAM, approve a specific exchange or guarantee that a product will be permitted. The CFTC must still review comments, draft any proposed rules, address legal and economic questions, and publish a final rule before the framework becomes operational.

Nor would the notice settle every jurisdictional question. The CFTC’s authority is not the same as the Securities and Exchange Commission’s authority. A crypto asset can raise different questions depending on its characteristics, the transaction, the promises made to purchasers and the service being offered. A CFTC registration would not automatically make every asset or every activity legal under every other federal or state regime.

## The market is watching a process, not a price catalyst

Bitcoin traded around the mid-0,000s on October 5 after approaching 7,000 and then reversing. US spot bitcoin ETFs had recorded a third consecutive weekly inflow, with roughly 41 million added in the latest week according to SoSoValue data cited by market reports. At the same time, long-dated Treasury yields remained elevated and the market was sensitive to the Federal Reserve’s next communications, including the October 7 release of the minutes from the September meeting.

Those numbers help explain the day’s market tension, but the CFTC notice is not a short-term price signal. It is a market-structure development. If the proposal becomes a workable final regime, its effects would likely show up over time through exchange registrations, product design, surveillance standards, margin practices and custody arrangements — not necessarily through an immediate move in bitcoin.

The next hard date is the comment deadline, which begins only after the notice appears in the Federal Register. Market participants should read the actual rulemaking document and track the CFTC docket rather than rely on slogans about “clarity.” The agency has opened a door; it has not yet finished building the room behind it.

## What to watch next

Three details will matter most as the process advances. First, the final definition of a CTX will determine which retail products fall inside the framework. Second, the capital, margin, segregation and proof-of-reserves requirements will show whether customer protection is substantive or mostly procedural. Third, the interaction with SEC rules and state licensing will determine whether a CAM reduces fragmentation in practice.

For American customers, the immediate consequence is simple: more documents, more comments and more waiting before any new federal crypto-market pathway becomes real. The important development is that the CFTC has moved from broad promises of future clarity to a named regulatory proposal with a public process attached to it.
