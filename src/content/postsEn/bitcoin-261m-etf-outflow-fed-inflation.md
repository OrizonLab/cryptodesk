Bitcoin is no longer trading on a crypto-only story. After a hotter U.S. inflation report, a sharp producer-price jump and a $261.4 million one-day withdrawal from U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs, the market is being forced to answer a more familiar Wall Street question: how much risk can investors carry when the Federal Reserve may have less room to ease?

The answer is showing up in the tape. Bitcoin briefly fell below $80,000 on Thursday before recovering above that level, while U.S. stocks and Treasury yields swung sharply. The move matters less as a prediction of Bitcoin’s next price than as a snapshot of how quickly institutional demand can reverse when inflation, rates and positioning collide.

## The inflation shock changed the rate conversation

The Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that the Consumer Price Index rose 0.6% in September on a seasonally adjusted basis. Prices were 3.7% higher than a year earlier, up from 3.4% in August. Core CPI, which excludes food and energy, rose 2.6% year over year after 2.4% in August.

The energy component did much of the immediate work: the BLS said energy prices increased 5.2% in September and were up 20.4% over 12 months. That does not automatically mean every underlying price pressure is accelerating, but it makes the path back to the Federal Reserve’s 2% inflation objective more difficult.

Thursday’s producer-price report added another layer. The PPI for final demand jumped 1.3% in September, the largest monthly increase since March 2022. On an unadjusted annual basis, final-demand prices were up 6.3%, while final-demand services rose 0.8% during the month. Producer prices are not consumer prices, and they do not pass through one-for-one. They do, however, give markets another reason to question how quickly inflation can cool.

That is why rate expectations moved so violently. Market pricing for an October hike briefly approached 50% after the CPI release, according to market reports, before retreating toward the high-20s to around 30%. The remaining debate is not simply hike versus cut. It is whether the Fed can hold rates steady without signaling that higher inflation will last longer than investors expected.

## ETF flows are the real institutional stress test

U.S. spot bitcoin ETFs recorded roughly $261.4 million in net outflows on Wednesday, according to SoSoValue data cited by crypto-market reporting. Fidelity’s FBTC accounted for about $152.7 million of the redemptions, while BlackRock’s IBIT saw approximately $110.9 million leave the fund. Bitwise’s BITB posted a small inflow, but it was not enough to offset the withdrawals.

The reversal was notable because the same group of products had taken in about $175.7 million on Tuesday. One positive day followed by a larger negative day is not proof that institutional investors have abandoned bitcoin. It is evidence that the marginal buyer is sensitive to macro risk.

The weekly picture is also turning defensive. Through Wednesday, reported net outflows for the week stood near $151 million, following roughly $681 million of withdrawals during the prior week. Ether ETFs added pressure, losing about $129.5 million on Wednesday and roughly $190 million for the week at that point.

Flows should be read carefully. A fund redemption can reflect portfolio rebalancing, tax planning, leverage reduction or a need to raise cash. It is not a survey of every holder’s long-term conviction. Still, spot ETFs are one of the clearest public channels through which regulated U.S. capital expresses demand. When flows reverse immediately after a macro shock, the signal is useful even if it is not permanent.

## Why Bitcoin moved with stocks, bonds and gold

Thursday’s market action looked broader than a crypto selloff. Bitcoin, copper and equity futures weakened, while gold gained and the 10-year Treasury yield fell back from a recent high. That combination points to a risk-reduction episode: investors were trimming growth-sensitive exposure while seeking assets that can absorb uncertainty.

Bitcoin does not behave like a traditional safe haven in every session. Its liquidity, leverage and round-the-clock trading make it especially responsive to changes in the dollar, real yields and futures positioning. A lower Treasury yield can help risk assets, but the reason for the decline matters. If yields fall because investors fear weaker growth or a broader market shock, the usual “lower rates are good for Bitcoin” shortcut can fail.

The U.S. market also has an event risk ahead. The Federal Open Market Committee is scheduled to meet on October 27–28. Between now and then, inflation expectations, Treasury yields and ETF creations will matter more than any single social-media narrative. A sustained inflow after a volatile macro week would suggest that allocators are buying weakness. Continued redemptions would show that the new risk regime is still dominating.

## What the prediction market signal can—and cannot—say

Prediction-market pricing supplied to CryptoDesk shows traders assigning very high probabilities to Bitcoin remaining above $72,000 and $74,000 on October 16, while the probability of Bitcoin clearing $82,000 on that date rose from 43.0% to 69.5% over 24 hours. Similar $82,000 contracts for October 17 and 18 also moved higher.

Those prices are useful as a sentiment gauge, not as a forecast or a trading instruction. They can move with thin liquidity, hedging and short-dated positioning. The notable point is the dispersion: markets can assign high odds to staying above lower thresholds while still treating a higher recovery level as uncertain. That is consistent with a market trying to stabilize, not one that has received a clean all-clear.

## The takeaway for U.S. readers

The immediate story is not that inflation guarantees a Federal Reserve hike, nor that one ETF outflow day proves a structural exit. The story is that macro data is once again setting the terms for crypto liquidity.

Bitcoin is being tested at the intersection of three forces: consumer inflation at 3.7%, producer prices rising 6.3% year over year, and institutional flows that can switch direction within 24 hours. The next useful evidence will be the durability of ETF demand and the Fed’s reaction function—not a dramatic headline built around one candle.
